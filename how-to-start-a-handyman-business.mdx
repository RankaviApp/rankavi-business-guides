Home repair spending has stayed strong as aging housing stock pushes homeowners toward smaller, faster fixes. A handyman business is attractive because you can start with tools you already own and grow revenue through repeat customers. It also carries a lower entry barrier than a licensed trade like plumbing. Just stay within the legal scope of general repair work.

## Defining Your Core Services

Look at what's already offered by handyman services on Thumbtack, Angi, and Facebook Marketplace. Note gaps like slow response times or limited availability. Decide on your core service mix, such as drywall repair, furniture assembly, or minor electrical fixes. Confirm steady local demand by talking to homeowners and property managers.

## Writing a Business Plan

Define your service radius, core skill set, and pricing model, whether hourly or flat-rate. Estimate how many jobs per week you need to hit your income target after drive time and materials. Plan how you'll track jobs, quotes, and invoices as your customer list grows.

## Setting Up Quoting and Scheduling Systems

A simple job management app pays for itself quickly once you're juggling multiple quotes and jobs each week. Look for one that handles quoting, invoicing, and scheduling in a single place, since switching between tools wastes time. Photo documentation of before-and-after work protects you from disputes and doubles as marketing material. Track your material costs by job so you know which types of work are actually most profitable. Many handymen underestimate how much time this kind of light admin saves once their calendar fills up.

## Registering the Business and Getting an EIN

Most handymen start as sole proprietors. An LLC is worth the modest cost given the property and injury risks in this work. Register your LLC with your state and apply for a free EIN from the IRS. A DBA filing lets you operate under a different business name if you stay a sole proprietor.

## Understanding Licensing Limits for Handyman Work

This is where handyman businesses most often get in trouble. Most states cap the dollar value of a job you can perform without a general contractor's license. That cap commonly runs $500 to $5,000. Above that threshold, or for structural, electrical, or plumbing work, you typically need a licensed contractor. Many states and cities also require a general business license or specific handyman registration.

## Insurance and Liability

General liability insurance is essential, since you're working inside people's homes daily. Workers' compensation becomes mandatory in most states once you hire employees, even part-time help. Some clients, particularly property managers, ask for a certificate of insurance before booking a job.

## Typical Startup Costs

A basic handyman business can start for $1,500 to $5,000. A quality tool set runs $1,000 to $3,000 for hand tools, power tools, and a ladder. A work van or truck is often your biggest expense if you don't already have one. Liability insurance typically costs $500 to $1,200 a year, and a scheduling app adds a small monthly fee.

## Funding Options

Most handyman businesses are self-funded, since tools and a vehicle are often already owned before the business starts. An equipment loan or business line of credit is common for a work van or larger tool investment. Starting part-time while keeping other income can fund your early tool and insurance costs.

## Pricing Your Services

Hourly rates typically run $50 to $100 depending on your market and skill specialty. Many handymen charge a minimum service call fee of $75 to $150 to cover drive time. Flat-rate pricing works well for common jobs like TV mounting, since customers prefer knowing the total upfront. Always price in material markup and travel time, not just labor.

## Finding Your First Customers

Nextdoor and local Facebook groups are highly effective, since homeowners actively ask for handyman recommendations there. List your business on Thumbtack, Angi, and Google Business Profile. Property managers and real estate agents are excellent recurring referral sources. Leave door hangers at completed jobs in neighborhoods where you want more work.

## Building a Reputation Through Reviews

Reviews carry outsized weight in the handyman trade, since most customers hire based on trust rather than price alone. Ask satisfied customers directly for a Google review right after completing a job, while the experience is still fresh. Respond professionally to every review, including negative ones, since prospective customers read how you handle criticism too. Before-and-after photos of completed jobs, shared on your Google Business Profile and social pages, convert new leads well. Few other marketing efforts do more.

## Deciding Which Jobs to Take and Which to Refer

Not every job that comes your way is worth taking, and knowing when to say no protects your reputation. Jobs requiring a licensed trade beyond your legal scope should be referred out, not stretched to fit. Building relationships with a licensed electrician, plumber, and roofer gives you people to refer, and they'll often refer work back. Low-margin jobs far outside your service area usually cost more in drive time than they're worth. Being selective, rather than accepting every lead, actually improves your reputation and your hourly earnings over time.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Taking on jobs above your state's licensing cap is a common and costly early mistake. Underquoting to win a job, without factoring drive time and material markup, quietly erodes your margin. Skipping a written estimate before starting work leads to disputes over scope and final price. New handymen also often neglect basic bookkeeping, which makes tax season and pricing decisions much harder later.

## Hiring and Scaling When You're Ready

Once booked out more than a week or two, bring on a second handyman or apprentice for smaller jobs. Standardize your quoting and scheduling process so a growing team doesn't create chaos. Many handyman businesses eventually specialize into a niche, like rental turnovers, once they have volume to be selective.

## Managing Seasonal Demand

Handyman demand often shifts with the seasons, with spring and fall bringing a surge in exterior and maintenance requests. Winter can slow down outdoor work but often brings interior projects and holiday-related repairs instead. Plan your marketing push around these predictable cycles rather than assuming demand stays flat year-round. Building a small reserve during busy months helps smooth out slower stretches without financial stress. Some handymen add seasonal specialties, like gutter cleaning in fall, to keep steady work through the year.
