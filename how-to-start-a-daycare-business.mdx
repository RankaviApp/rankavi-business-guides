Childcare demand consistently outpaces supply in many communities. Working parents often face long waitlists for quality care near their home or workplace. Starting a daycare is more heavily regulated than most small businesses. That regulation also protects operators from low-quality, unlicensed competition undercutting on price. A well-run daycare can build a loyal, referral-driven client base quickly. Parents trust word of mouth far more than advertising.

## Home-Based or Licensed Center

Decide whether you'll operate a home-based daycare or work toward a licensed center. The two paths have very different regulatory requirements, capacity limits, and startup costs. A home-based program is typically faster and cheaper to launch. A licensed center offers a higher revenue ceiling but demands far more upfront capital. Talk to local parents about what they wish existing options offered. That might be extended hours, infant care, or a specific educational approach.

## Licensing and Required Certifications

Daycare licensing is state-regulated and typically requires a background check, home or facility inspection, and CPR and first aid certification. You'll also need to complete state-mandated childcare training hours before enrolling a single child. Home-based daycares usually have lower thresholds than licensed centers. Both require ongoing compliance with staff-to-child ratios and health and safety codes. Fire marshal inspections and health department approval are common requirements before you can legally open.

## Registering Your Business and Getting an EIN

Form an LLC to protect personal assets, which matters given the duty of care involved in childcare. There's serious liability exposure if an incident occurs. Apply for an EIN through the IRS website, required for licensing applications, banking, and hiring staff. Open a dedicated business account to track tuition income and operating expenses separately from personal finances. This also simplifies any state audits of your program.

## Insurance Requirements

General liability insurance is essential given the direct supervision of children involved. There's real financial exposure from any accident on your premises. Many states require specific childcare liability coverage as a licensing condition, distinct from a standard homeowner's policy. If you're hiring staff, workers' compensation insurance becomes mandatory in nearly every state once you have employees.

## Estimating Your Startup Costs

A home-based daycare requires childproofing, safety equipment, educational materials, and outdoor play space improvements to meet licensing standards. A licensed center involves far higher costs, including facility lease or purchase. Commercial kitchen equipment adds more if meals are served on-site. You'll also need furniture sized appropriately for the age groups you serve. Licensing fees, background checks, and required training courses add to the upfront investment regardless of which path you choose.

## Funding Options for New Providers

Many home-based daycare operators self-fund with personal savings given lower startup costs compared to a full commercial center. Center-based daycares often require an SBA loan or commercial loan given the facility investment involved. Lenders will want to see a solid enrollment projection before approving financing. Some states and nonprofits offer small business grants specifically for childcare providers given ongoing shortages in the sector.

## Setting Tuition Rates

Price based on local market rates for your age groups. Infant care commands higher tuition than care for older children. Stricter required ratios and more intensive supervision explain the gap. Weekly or monthly tuition models are standard, sometimes with a registration fee to secure a waitlist spot. Research competitor pricing but ensure your rates cover staffing costs adequately, since labor is typically the largest ongoing expense.

## Filling Your Enrollment

Local parent Facebook groups and community boards are often the fastest way to reach families actively searching for care. Partnerships with local pediatricians, churches, and employers can generate steady referrals from trusted community sources. Word of mouth is exceptionally powerful in childcare, so treat your first families exceptionally well. As enrollment grows, hire additional qualified staff to maintain required ratios and expand capacity without compromising care quality.

## Staffing Ratios and Hiring Qualified Caregivers

State-mandated staff-to-child ratios tighten significantly for infants and loosen gradually as children get older. This directly shapes your labor costs at every age group. Background checks and often a minimum age or education requirement apply to anyone working directly with children. Build hiring timelines around how long those checks take to clear. Retaining good staff matters enormously in childcare, since turnover disrupts the consistency parents are paying for.

## Curriculum and Daily Structure

Even a home-based program benefits from a consistent daily schedule that balances structured learning with free play and rest time. Parents increasingly ask about specific educational philosophies, whether that's Montessori-inspired activities or simple school-readiness basics. A clear, written daily schedule shared with parents builds confidence in your program. It also reduces pickup-time questions about how the day went.

## Meals, Naps, and Health Protocols

Many states require licensed providers to follow specific nutrition guidelines if meals are included in tuition. Check whether you'll need a food program certification. Nap schedules and safe sleep practices for infants carry their own strict regulatory requirements, particularly around crib safety. A clear illness policy, covering when a sick child must stay home, protects both your other families and your staff.

## Common Mistakes New Providers Make

Underestimating how long licensing approval takes is one of the most common mistakes. Inspections and background checks can stretch well beyond initial expectations. Setting tuition too low to fill spots quickly often becomes difficult to correct later without upsetting existing families. Failing to build a substitute staffing plan for illness or emergencies can force an unexpected closure day.

## Building Parent Communication Systems

Consistent daily updates, whether a simple app or a written note, reassure parents their child had a good day. Many providers now use a childcare app that sends photos, meal notes, and nap times directly to parents. This kind of transparency builds trust quickly, especially with parents enrolling their first child in outside care. A clear policy for handling parent concerns or complaints keeps small issues from becoming enrollment losses.

## Planning for Growth and a Waitlist

Once you're consistently full, start a formal waitlist system rather than turning families away informally. A waitlist gives you real data on demand. That data helps you decide whether to expand capacity or open a second location. Charging a small waitlist deposit filters out families who aren't seriously committed. Reviewing your waitlist regularly also helps you plan staffing needs before a spot actually opens.
