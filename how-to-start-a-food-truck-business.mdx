The US food truck industry generates several billion dollars in annual revenue. It keeps expanding as cities loosen mobile vending rules. It offers lower startup costs than a full restaurant while still letting owners build a loyal local following. A strong concept and consistent locations can turn a single truck into a recognizable local brand quickly.

## Food Truck Licensing, Permits, and Health Inspections

You will need a mobile food vendor permit from your city. You also need a health department permit that requires passing a kitchen inspection. A food handler's certification is required for you and often for each employee working on the truck. Fire safety inspection is typically required due to propane tanks and cooking equipment onboard. Many cities also require a separate parking or vending permit for each specific location you plan to operate from. Commissary kitchen agreements are often mandatory. Most health departments require trucks to be based out of a licensed commercial kitchen overnight. Some cities cap the number of mobile vending permits issued each year, so apply early. Keep all permits visible on the truck, since health inspectors can perform surprise checks during service.

## Choosing and Validating Your Concept

Choose a food concept that is fast to prepare, travels well, and stands out from existing local trucks. Study which cuisines and dishes already have trucks nearby, and look for a gap you can fill. Test your menu at a friend's event or a small pop-up before committing to a truck purchase. Confirm your pricing supports a profitable margin once food, fuel, and staffing costs are included.

## Typical Startup Costs

A used food truck with kitchen equipment already installed typically costs $50,000 to $100,000. A new custom-built truck can run $100,000 to $175,000 depending on equipment and finish quality. Initial food inventory and smallwares usually cost $2,000 to $5,000 for your first weeks of service. Add $3,000 to $10,000 for permits, insurance, and a point-of-sale system before your first service day. Commissary kitchen rental typically costs $300 to $1,000 a month depending on your city and usage. Generator and propane costs add another $1,000 to $3,000 depending on your equipment needs. Budget for regular vehicle maintenance, since a breakdown directly shuts down your ability to earn revenue. A backup generator or extra propane tank is worth the cost, since running out mid-service means turning customers away. Branded wraps and signage for the truck typically cost $2,000 to $5,000 but pay off through recognition at repeat locations. Many owners also budget for a folding menu board and simple point-of-sale hardware. Faster ordering during rushes directly translates into more sales per hour. A small awning or window setup improves the customer experience while waiting in line. Some operators also budget for branded napkins or cups, which reinforce the brand at every touchpoint.

## Writing a Business Plan

Define your core menu, target locations, and typical operating schedule, such as lunch service near office parks. Estimate your daily break-even sales based on food cost, staffing, and truck expenses. Plan which events, festivals, and regular spots you will target for consistent revenue. Set a launch timeline that accounts for permitting, which often takes longer than expected.

## Registering Your Business and Getting an EIN

An LLC is standard for food trucks given the liability involved in food service and vehicle operation. Register your business with your state and apply for a free EIN through the IRS. This protects your personal assets from claims related to foodborne illness or accidents. Open a business bank account to manage the mix of cash and card payments food trucks typically handle.

## Funding Your Launch

Equipment financing specifically for food trucks is available through lenders familiar with the industry. SBA loans work well for owners with some savings and a solid business plan. Some owners start with a trailer instead of a full truck to reduce initial investment. Reinvest early revenue into a second location schedule or additional staff once cash flow is proven.

## Pricing Your Menu

Calculate your food cost percentage per dish and aim to keep it around a third of the menu price. Factor in fuel, commissary kitchen fees, and staffing when setting your final prices. Price competitively against similar trucks in your market, but do not underprice a differentiated concept. Adjust pricing for festival and event bookings, which often carry different commission structures than street service.

## Where to Find Your First Customers

Social media, especially Instagram and TikTok, is critical for food trucks since daily location posts drive traffic directly. Food truck locator apps and city vendor directories help new customers discover you. Booking local festivals, breweries, and office parks builds a predictable weekly schedule. A loyal following often comes from consistent quality and a recognizable brand more than any single ad. Partnering with local breweries or event venues for regular weekly slots builds predictable repeat traffic. Text or email alerts for regulars can drive strong turnout when you announce a new location.

## Growing and Hiring a Crew

Hire a second crew member once volume regularly exceeds what one or two people can handle during service. Consider a second truck once your first route consistently sells out at multiple stops. Catering and private events add higher-margin revenue using the same kitchen and staff. Track sales by location and day to build the most profitable weekly schedule. Standardizing prep recipes and portion sizes makes it easier to train new staff consistently. Revisit your route regularly, since a location that worked at launch can fade over time.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Underestimating permitting timelines is one of the most common mistakes new food truck owners make. Approvals often take longer than expected. Overbuilding the menu can slow service and increase food waste, especially for a small crew working in a tight space. Skipping a commissary kitchen agreement can delay your health inspection and push back your launch date. Ignoring vehicle maintenance is risky, since a breakdown on a scheduled event day directly costs you revenue and reputation.
