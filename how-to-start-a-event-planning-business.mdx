Event planning remains a resilient service business because weddings, corporate events, and milestone celebrations happen regardless of economic conditions. Many planners start solo with minimal overhead, charging a percentage of event budgets or flat coordination fees. The barrier to entry is low, but building trust with clients and vendors takes deliberate effort.

## Choosing Your Event Planning Niche

Pick a niche early, whether that is weddings, corporate events, or social celebrations like birthdays and anniversaries. Each niche has a different sales cycle and client expectation set. Weddings often book a year in advance, while corporate events can move on a much tighter timeline.

Research local competitors and note their pricing structure and typical package inclusions. Talk to venue owners about which planning gaps they see most often from clients. A focused niche makes your portfolio and marketing far more effective than trying to serve every event type at once.

Social events like milestone birthdays and baby showers often have shorter planning windows. They also require less coordination complexity than a wedding. This can make them a good starting niche while you build your vendor relationships and portfolio.

## Registering the Business and Getting an EIN

Forming an LLC protects your personal assets from contract disputes or event-day liability claims. Register your LLC with your state and apply for a free EIN through the IRS. Use it to open a business bank account. Many venues and vendors will also want to see your business registration before referring clients your way.

Opening a separate business checking account under your EIN also makes deposits easier to track. Commingling client funds with personal money creates accounting headaches and potential legal exposure.

## Licensing, Permits, and Insurance

Event planning itself typically does not require a special state license, but general business licensing applies in most cities. General liability insurance is essential and often required by venues before they will allow you to coordinate on-site. If you plan to serve alcohol or handle food logistics directly, additional local permits may apply.

Check with any venue you work with regularly about their specific vendor requirements. Some require proof of insurance before your first walkthrough. If you plan to handle event deposits or payments for clients, think through the structure carefully. Staying compliant matters here.

## Startup Costs to Plan For

Starting an event planning business is relatively affordable, often $2,000 to $5,000 total. That covers a website, portfolio shoot, software, and initial marketing. Event planning and CRM software subscriptions typically run $30 to $100 per month. Many planners build their portfolio by offering discounted rates on a few early events. They raise prices once their skill level justifies it.

Budget a small amount for professional liability coverage as well. Some venues will not sign off without proof of insurance, regardless of your event size.

A professional portfolio shoot is worth prioritizing, whether styled or from real early events. Visuals drive most booking decisions in this industry.

## Funding Your Launch

Most event planners self-fund their launch given the relatively low upfront costs compared to product-based businesses. A small business credit card can cover early software and marketing expenses while you build a client base. Partnering with an established planner as a subcontractor first is common. It lets you earn while learning the business before going fully independent.

## Setting Your Pricing

Full-service wedding planning commonly ranges from 10 to 20 percent of the total event budget. Smaller events often get a flat fee instead. Day-of coordination is typically priced as a flat rate, often $800 to $2,500 depending on your market. Corporate event pricing tends to be hourly or project-based, reflecting the scope of logistics.

Offer tiered packages, from full planning down to month-of coordination. This lets clients choose by budget without you undercutting your own rates.

## Writing Contracts and Cancellation Terms

A written contract protects both you and your client from misunderstandings later. Spell out exactly what services are included, and what counts as an add-on. Define your cancellation policy clearly, including how much of the deposit is refundable and by when. Many planners use a sliding scale, where the refund percentage drops as the event date approaches.

Include a force majeure clause covering weather, venue closures, or other events outside your control. This protects you if an event has to be postponed for reasons neither party caused. Having a lawyer review your template contract once is worth the cost. It catches gaps you might miss on your own.

## Building Your Vendor Network

Your vendor relationships become one of your most valuable assets over time. Building relationships with venues, photographers, and caterers creates a referral pipeline that flows in both directions. A trusted vendor list also lets you negotiate better rates and availability for your clients, which strengthens your value proposition.

Attending vendor mixers and industry association events helps you meet people beyond your existing network. A shared spreadsheet or CRM tool tracking each vendor's pricing and availability helps too. Logging past client feedback keeps your recommendations sharp as the list grows.

## Finding Your First Clients

Instagram and Pinterest are essential for showcasing past events visually, since clients hire based on aesthetic and vibe. Bridal shows and local business networking events help you meet clients directly before they book a planner. Offering to coordinate a friend's event at a reduced rate can generate your first portfolio pieces and testimonials.

Ask every early client for a short written testimonial and permission to share photos. Real client feedback builds trust faster than any styled photoshoot.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Underpricing your time on full-service packages is a frequent early mistake that is hard to correct with existing clients. Failing to put contract terms and cancellation policies in writing can lead to costly disputes. Many new planners also take on too many events at once before building a reliable vendor and assistant network.

Booking two large weddings on the same weekend without a second coordinator is a common overreach. It damages both client experiences at once.

## Hiring and Scaling Your Team

Once you are consistently turning away bookings, bring on a part-time assistant planner to handle logistics on event day. Specializing further, such as destination weddings or corporate conferences, can justify premium pricing. Building a trusted vendor directory becomes a valuable asset over time. You can eventually license or share it with junior planners.

Train an assistant on your specific communication style with clients. This keeps the experience consistent as you delegate more coordination.
