Painting is one of the most consistently in-demand home improvement services. Interior and exterior paint jobs need refreshing every five to ten years. Startup costs are manageable compared to many trades, and skilled painters can move quickly from solo contractor to running crews. Repeat business and referrals drive much of the industry's steady growth.

## Assessing Local Demand

Look at how many painting contractors already serve your area and whether they focus on residential, commercial, or both. Check reviews for common complaints like missed deadlines or sloppy prep work, since quality gaps are easy to exploit. Ask a few local real estate agents about painting demand tied to home sales and staging.

New construction growth in your area can also signal steady demand from builders needing interior painting on a recurring schedule.

## Registering the Business and Getting an EIN

An LLC is the standard choice for painting contractors given the property damage and liability risk involved in this work. Register your LLC with your state and apply for a free EIN from the IRS. Use it to open a business bank account. Many commercial clients and property managers will require proof of this registration before hiring you.

Set up a separate account for materials purchases too. It makes it easier to track true job costs against each bid.

## Contractor Licensing and Insurance

Many states require a contractor's license for painting work above a certain dollar threshold. Check your state contractor licensing board. General liability insurance is essential, since painting involves working around client property, furniture, and landscaping that can be damaged.

Some states also require passing a trade exam before a contractor license is issued. Others accept proof of prior work experience instead.

If your crew works on pre-1978 homes, EPA lead-safe certification is federally required for any surface disturbance. Some cities also require a local business license separate from state contractor licensing. Larger commercial jobs may require a performance bond.

## Equipment and Startup Costs

A basic painting setup with ladders, sprayers, brushes, and drop cloths can start around $2,000 to $5,000. A commercial-grade airless sprayer alone can run $500 to $1,500 depending on capacity. Budget separately for a reliable work vehicle, insurance, and enough working capital to cover materials before client payment arrives.

Quality drop cloths, tape, and masking materials seem minor but protect you from costly damage claims. Skimping on these adds real risk for very little savings.

Scaffolding or lift equipment becomes necessary once you take on two-story exteriors or commercial buildings. This adds meaningfully to your budget.

## Choosing a Reliable Work Vehicle

Your work vehicle needs to haul ladders, sprayers, and enough materials for a full day of work. Many painters start with a used cargo van rather than buying new, since resale value matters less than reliability. Interior racking and shelving keep equipment organized and protect it during transport.

Wrapping your vehicle with your business name and phone number turns every job site into free advertising. Neighbors and passersby often call after seeing a parked vehicle at a job they admired.

## Funding Your Equipment

Many painters self-fund their initial equipment purchase using savings from prior employment in the trade. Equipment financing through a local bank or supplier can spread out the cost. This works well for a commercial sprayer or a work van. A business line of credit helps smooth cash flow between paying for materials and collecting payment on completed jobs.

Some paint suppliers also offer contractor accounts with net-30 terms. These effectively function as short-term financing for your material costs.

## Choosing the Right Paint and Materials

Using a mid-grade or premium paint line reduces callbacks from uneven coverage or early fading. Many contractors negotiate contractor pricing directly with a paint supplier once they are buying in volume regularly. Match the right paint sheen to the surface, like satin for trim versus flat for ceilings. This affects both durability and client satisfaction.

## Pricing Painting Jobs

Interior painting is commonly priced per room or per square foot, factoring in ceiling height and trim complexity. Exterior jobs are typically priced by square footage of siding, with adjustments for prep work like scraping or power washing. Research three to five local competitors and position your pricing based on your experience level and warranty offering.

Offering a multi-year warranty on exterior work can justify a premium price against competitors who offer none.

## Finding Your First Clients

Door hangers and yard signs at completed job sites remain effective for generating leads in the surrounding neighborhood. Partnering with real estate agents for pre-listing paint jobs creates a steady referral channel. Google Business Profile listings with strong reviews and before-and-after photos matter heavily for local search visibility.

Bidding on smaller commercial jobs, like office suites or retail buildouts, can diversify your pipeline beyond seasonal residential demand.

## Estimating and Bidding Accurately

A written estimate protects you from scope creep and payment disputes. Measure square footage carefully, and account for extra coats on dark colors or bare drywall. Many contractors add a contingency line for unexpected repairs found once prep work begins. Rotted trim or badly peeling old paint often turns up.

Walk every job site in person before quoting, even for a job that sounds simple over the phone. Photos from the client rarely show the full condition of a wall or ceiling. Breaking your bid into labor, materials, and any add-on services helps clients understand exactly what they are paying for. This transparency also reduces requests to lower your price later.

## Mistakes That Cost New Contractors

Skipping proper surface prep to save time often leads to peeling and callbacks that damage your reputation. Underestimating paint and labor on a bid can turn a profitable job into a loss. Many new contractors also neglect lead-safe certification requirements, risking fines on older homes.

## Building Your Crew and Scaling

Once you are consistently booked several weeks out, hiring your first crew member lets you take on multiple jobs simultaneously. Standardizing your estimating process ensures consistent pricing as you delegate quoting to crew leads. Adding commercial contracts or new construction accounts can provide larger, more predictable revenue as you scale beyond residential work.

Investing in a second sprayer and van sooner rather than later prevents one crew's downtime from stalling your entire schedule.
