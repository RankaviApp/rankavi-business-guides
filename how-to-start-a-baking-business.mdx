Home baking businesses have grown steadily as cottage food laws have expanded across most states, lowering the barrier to entry. Many bakers start part-time from a home kitchen before ever renting commercial space. The appeal is real: low starting costs and flexible hours. Customers will also pay a premium for a product that looks homemade.

## Picking Your Baking Niche

Decide what you actually want to bake and sell. Custom cakes, cookies, bread, and pastries each have different customer bases and margins. Custom cakes often carry the highest per-order revenue but require design skill and more time per piece. Cookies and bars scale more easily for wholesale and bulk orders.

Bread baking has a loyal following but requires longer production windows and often thinner margins per loaf. Consider your own schedule and kitchen capacity first. Some product lines demand overnight proofing or multiple daily bakes.

Look at local farmers markets and Facebook marketplace groups to see what is already being sold nearby. Ask friends and neighbors to taste-test your signature items and give honest feedback. Do this before you invest in packaging or a website. A clear specialty makes your marketing far easier than trying to offer everything at once.

## Cottage Food Licensing and Permits

Most states allow home bakers to sell certain non-hazardous baked goods under a cottage food law, without a commercial kitchen. Requirements vary widely, so check your state health department for the specific allowed products, sales limits, and labeling rules. Items requiring refrigeration, like cream-filled pastries, usually need a licensed commercial kitchen instead.

A food handler's permit is commonly required even under cottage food rules. Some states cap your annual cottage food revenue, which can force a move to a commercial kitchen sooner than expected. Labeling requirements typically include your business name, ingredients, and an allergen statement on every package.

## Registering the Business and Getting an EIN

Many home bakers start as a sole proprietorship, but forming an LLC adds liability protection once selling regularly. Register with your state and apply for a free EIN from the IRS. Use this to open a business bank account. Keeping business and personal finances separate makes tax season much simpler and looks more professional to wholesale clients.

## Startup Costs to Expect

A cottage food baking business can often start for $500 to $2,000, covering an oven upgrade, mixers, pans, and packaging. If your products require a commercial kitchen, renting one by the hour typically runs $15 to $40. Budget for ingredients, custom labels, and a scale accurate enough for consistent recipes.

A professional stand mixer alone can run $300 to $600, and specialty cake decorating tools add up quickly. Many bakers start with home equipment and reinvest early profits into commercial-grade tools as order volume increases. Photography props and simple packaging also deserve a line in your budget, since presentation drives repeat orders.

## Funding Your Bakery

Most bakers self-fund with personal savings since equipment needs are modest at the cottage food stage. A small personal loan or credit card float can cover a mixer upgrade or a first batch of custom packaging. Microloans through community development lenders are worth checking if you plan to move into a commercial kitchen quickly. That transition often requires a larger jump in capital.

## Pricing Your Baked Goods

Price by calculating ingredient cost, then multiplying by three to five times to cover labor, overhead, and profit. Custom cakes should be priced per serving plus a design fee for intricate work. Compare your prices to at least three local competitors so you are not underpricing your time. Underpricing is the most common mistake new bakers make.

Seasonal items, like holiday cookies or specialty cakes, can often command premium pricing due to limited availability. Consider offering tiered packages for events, since a fixed menu simplifies quoting and keeps your production line predictable.

Raise prices gradually as your skills and demand grow rather than waiting until you are overwhelmed. Many bakers set a review date every few months to check ingredient costs against current pricing. Flour, butter, and sugar prices shift often.

## Where to Find Your First Customers

Instagram and TikTok are the primary discovery channels for custom baked goods, since photos and videos sell the product directly. Local farmers markets let people taste your work before committing to a custom order. Partnering with a local coffee shop for wholesale pastries builds steady weekly income. Word of mouth from a great wedding or birthday cake can carry a bakery for years.

A simple online ordering form removes friction for customers ready to buy. Even a basic Google form linked from your Instagram bio works. Responding quickly to inquiries matters more than people expect. Custom cake buyers often message several bakers at once and book whoever replies first.

Joining a local mom's group or community event as a vendor helps too. That audience is already inclined to order for parties. Offering a small tasting box to a few local influencers can generate reviews that outlast a paid ad campaign.

## Mistakes to Avoid Early On

Underpricing custom orders to build a portfolio is common but hard to correct once customers expect low prices. Skipping proper labeling under cottage food rules can result in fines or a shutdown order from your health department. Overcommitting to too many product lines early on stretches your kitchen time thin and hurts consistency.

Failing to track your annual cottage food sales limit is another easy mistake. Exceeding it without a commercial kitchen license can shut down your operation abruptly.

## Hiring and Scaling Your Bakery

As orders grow beyond what you can bake alone, bring on a part-time assistant for packaging and decorating. Moving into a licensed commercial kitchen opens up wholesale accounts and higher-volume products like refrigerated items. Consider adding online ordering and local delivery once demand is consistent enough to justify the logistics.

A shared commercial kitchen space is often a lower-cost first step than leasing your own storefront. It lets you test wholesale volume before a bigger commitment.
