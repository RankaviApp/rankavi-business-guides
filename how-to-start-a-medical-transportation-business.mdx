Non-emergency medical transportation, often called NEMT, serves patients who need rides to dialysis, therapy, and routine appointments. These patients do not need an ambulance. Demand is driven by an aging population and expanding Medicaid transportation benefits in many states. Operators who secure a Medicaid broker contract can build a stable, recurring revenue base quickly.

## Understanding Local Demand

Contact your state Medicaid transportation broker, such as ModivCare or Access2Care, to understand demand in your area. Talk to dialysis centers, nursing homes, and rehabilitation clinics about their current transportation gaps. Some regions are saturated with NEMT providers while others have long wait times. Local research matters more than national averages here.

Request ride volume data from your local Area Agency on Aging if available. These agencies often track unmet transportation needs among seniors in your county.

## Choosing Your Service Type

Decide whether you will focus on ambulatory patients, wheelchair transport, or stretcher transport. Each type requires different vehicles and staff training. Ambulatory service requires the least equipment investment. Stretcher transport commands higher per-trip rates but needs specialized vehicles and trained staff. Outline your target contracts, whether Medicaid, private insurance, or direct-pay patients through hospitals and clinics.

Many operators start with wheelchair transport alone. It covers a large share of dialysis and therapy demand without the added cost of stretcher equipment.

## Registering the Business and Getting an EIN

An LLC is standard for NEMT businesses given the liability involved in transporting medically vulnerable patients. Register your LLC with your state and apply for a free EIN from the IRS. This lets you open a business bank account. Medicaid brokers and insurance payers will require this registration before contracting with you.

## Licensing, Permits, and Insurance

NEMT businesses typically need a state transportation or ambulance service license, which varies by state. Drivers usually need background checks, a clean driving record, and CPR certification. Commercial auto insurance with higher liability limits than standard policies is required. Some states mandate specific NEMT insurance minimums.

Some states also require passenger assistance training for drivers, covering safe transfer techniques for wheelchair patients. This certification is usually required before a driver can operate.

You will also need to enroll as a Medicaid provider if you plan to bill Medicaid directly. That involves its own separate application process. Vehicle inspections are often required annually to maintain your operating license, particularly for wheelchair-accessible vans.

## Choosing Dispatch and Scheduling Software

Manual scheduling works for a single vehicle but breaks down fast once you add drivers. NEMT-specific dispatch software helps match trips to available vehicles based on wheelchair capacity and location. Many platforms also integrate directly with Medicaid broker systems, which speeds up trip confirmation.

Look for software with real-time GPS tracking, since brokers often require proof of on-time pickup. This data also protects you if a dispute arises over a missed appointment.

## Startup Costs for Vehicles and Equipment

A single wheelchair-accessible van typically costs $30,000 to $60,000 new, with used ADA-compliant vans available for less. Add costs for commercial insurance, background checks, and dispatch software. These can run several hundred dollars monthly. Many operators start with one or two vehicles before expanding once contracts are secured.

Ongoing costs like fuel, maintenance, and lift equipment servicing add up quickly with a wheelchair-accessible fleet. Building these into your rate negotiations with brokers upfront prevents thin margins later.

Stretcher-equipped vehicles cost considerably more due to the medical equipment involved. Many new operators begin with ambulatory and wheelchair service before adding stretcher capability.

## Funding Your Fleet

Commercial vehicle financing is the most common way to acquire ADA-compliant vans without paying the full cost upfront. Small Business Administration loans work well for operators with a business plan and some capital already committed. Some states offer small grants or workforce funding for transportation businesses serving underserved communities.

## Setting Your Trip Pricing

Medicaid and insurance contracts typically pay a set per-trip or per-mile rate, negotiated through the broker relationship. Private-pay and hospital discharge transport can be priced per trip, factoring in mileage and wait time. Wheelchair and stretcher transport commands higher rates than ambulatory rides due to specialized equipment.

## Hiring and Training Drivers

Your drivers directly represent your business to vulnerable patients, so hiring carefully matters. Look for candidates with a clean driving record, patience, and prior caregiving or transport experience. Run background checks that meet your state's specific NEMT requirements before onboarding anyone.

Training should cover more than driving skills alone. Cover safe wheelchair loading, basic first aid response, and how to communicate calmly with patients experiencing pain or anxiety. Many operators pair new drivers with an experienced one for their first two weeks. This mentoring period catches bad habits before they become routine.

Retention matters as much as hiring in this field. Driver turnover disrupts patient trust and increases your training costs repeatedly. Offering predictable schedules and mileage reimbursement helps keep good drivers longer.

## Building Referral Relationships

Applying to become an approved provider with your state's Medicaid broker is the single most important early step. Building relationships with hospital discharge planners and case managers generates consistent referral volume. Nursing homes and dialysis centers often need reliable recurring transportation partners.

Bringing a simple one-page flyer to discharge planning offices makes it easy for staff to refer patients. List your service area, vehicle types, and contact information clearly on it.

## Avoiding Common Pitfalls

Missing Medicaid billing deadlines or documentation requirements can delay payment for weeks. Understaffing your dispatch process leads to late pickups, which quickly damages your reputation with case managers. Many new operators also underestimate how much driver turnover affects service reliability.

Failing to double check appointment times before pickup is a small oversight with big consequences. It causes missed dialysis and therapy sessions, which brokers track closely.

## Hiring and Scaling Your Fleet

As trip volume grows, hire additional trained drivers and invest in dispatch software to manage scheduling. Adding stretcher or bariatric transport capability opens up higher-paying specialized trip categories. Expanding your broker relationships to neighboring counties can significantly increase trip volume.

Cross-training dispatchers to handle scheduling for multiple vehicle types keeps your operation flexible. Demand often shifts between ambulatory and wheelchair trips throughout the week.
