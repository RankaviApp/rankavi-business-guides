Hair care is a recurring, largely recession-resistant service. People keep getting haircuts and color touch-ups on a regular schedule regardless of the economy. Building a client base takes time, but stylists with strong technical skill can build a sustainable business relatively quickly. Chair rental, booth rental, and full salon ownership each offer different paths into this industry.

## Getting Licensed

Every practicing stylist needs a state cosmetology license. That requires completing an accredited program and passing state board exams. If you open a physical salon, you will also need a separate salon license. This comes from your state cosmetology board directly. Health department inspections are common for salons due to sanitation requirements around tools and chemical use. Confirm your state's continuing education requirements to keep your license active.

## Choosing Your Path: Chair Rental, Studio, or Salon

Decide early whether you want to rent a chair, open your own small studio, or eventually run a full salon. Study which services are underserved locally, such as curly hair specialists, color correction, or men's grooming. Talk to potential clients about what frustrates them with current salon options nearby. Your specialty and target clientele should shape every decision that follows.

## Startup Costs

Chair or booth rental is the lowest-cost entry point. It often requires only styling tools, product inventory, and a weekly or monthly rental fee. Opening your own studio or salon involves lease costs, station buildout, and plumbing for wash stations, which adds up quickly. Product inventory, a booking system, and initial marketing materials are needed regardless of format. Many stylists start with chair rental and reinvest earnings toward opening their own space later.

## Writing a Business Plan

Include your service menu, pricing structure, and a realistic client acquisition timeline in your plan. If you plan to open a physical salon, factor in build-out costs, station count, and staffing needs. Note your point of difference clearly, since generic full-service salons face heavy local competition. A clear plan helps if you need financing for a lease or renovation.

## Registering Your Business and Getting an EIN

Many independent stylists start as sole proprietors while renting a chair. An LLC is worth considering once you hire staff or sign a commercial lease. Register your business name with your state and apply for a free EIN through the IRS website. The LLC structure protects your personal assets from salon-related liability if you take on partners later.

## Funding Options

Chair rental stylists typically self-fund from personal savings since costs are modest. Opening a full salon often requires a small business loan or an SBA-backed loan given the leasehold improvements involved. Some stylists partner with an established salon owner to co-invest in a buildout. Vendor financing for salon equipment and furniture is another common option for a new studio.

## Setting Your Prices

Price your services based on your experience level and local market rates. Color and extensions are typically priced higher than a basic cut given the added time and product cost. Many stylists build tiered pricing as they gain experience and a loyal following. Offer package pricing for maintenance clients who return every four to six weeks. Review pricing regularly against product cost increases and local competitor rates.

## Equipment and Supplies

A basic kit includes shears, clippers, styling tools, and a station chair if renting an empty space. Color services require a full inventory of dyes, developers, and mixing tools from a professional supplier. Many stylists start with a smaller product line and expand as their service menu grows. Buying professional-grade tools upfront usually saves money over replacing cheaper ones repeatedly.

## Building Your Client Base

Instagram and TikTok before-and-after content remains one of the most effective channels for this industry. Ask friends and family for referrals when you are just starting. Consider discounted rates for your first several clients in exchange for reviews and photos. Partnering with local photographers or bridal vendors can open a steady stream of special-occasion clients. Building relationships with your salon's front desk staff also helps generate walk-in referrals.

## Growing Beyond One Chair

Once your own book is consistently full, consider renting an additional chair. Bring on a junior stylist you can train and mentor as demand grows. Scaling into a full salon means managing booking systems and inventory across multiple stylists. Many successful salon owners keep working behind the chair even as they grow. Track no-show rates and rebooking rates closely as you scale.

## Insurance and Liability

Most salons and chair renters carry general liability insurance for chemical reactions or accidental injury. Some booth rental agreements require proof of your own policy before you can start working. Professional liability coverage protects against claims tied to a botched color or chemical service. Ask your insurer specifically about coverage for the services you actually perform. Many policies exclude certain high-risk chemical treatments unless you add them by name, so read the fine print before an incident forces you to find out the hard way.

## Booth Rental vs. Commission vs. Owning Your Own Space

Many stylists start by renting a booth at an existing salon, which keeps upfront costs low but limits how much of the client relationship you control. Commission-based salons pay less per service but supply clients and product, which suits someone new to the trade. Opening your own space costs more but lets you set your own hours, pricing, and brand. Decide which model fits your stage before signing any lease or contract.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Undercharging early to build a client list often makes it hard to raise prices later. Skipping continuing education can put your license at risk and limit your legal services. Neglecting a no-show or cancellation policy costs stylists real income over time. Overinvesting in a full salon buildout before proving demand through chair rental is a common mistake.
