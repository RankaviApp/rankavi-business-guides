The US floral industry is worth billions of dollars annually, with weddings, events, and everyday gifting driving steady demand. Independent florists and home-based bouquet makers have carved out strong niches by offering personalized, locally sourced arrangements. Social media has made it easier than ever for a small bouquet business to build a following without a storefront.

## Florist Licensing and Permits

Most states do not require a special florist license, but a general business license and seller's permit are typically required. If you sell at farmers markets, you will need a vendor permit from the market organizer. Home-based flower businesses should check local cottage food or home occupation rules. Some cities restrict retail sales from residential addresses. For wedding and event work, liability insurance is strongly recommended in case of venue damage or delivery issues. Some venues require proof of insurance before allowing outside vendors to set up on-site. If you plan to deliver arrangements, check whether your vehicle insurance covers commercial use. Keep contracts and deposit agreements in writing for every wedding or large event booking.

## Choosing and Validating Your Niche

Decide whether you will focus on everyday bouquets, wedding florals, or subscription arrangements delivered on a schedule. Study local florists and Etsy sellers to understand pricing and popular styles in your area. Make sample bouquets and get feedback from friends or a small test group of buyers. Confirm demand through a soft launch before investing in a full flower inventory.

## Typical Startup Costs

Initial flower inventory and cooler storage can cost $500 to $2,000 depending on order volume. Basic tools like shears, floral foam, wire, and vases typically run $200 to $500. A small flower cooler or refrigerated storage unit, if needed, can add $500 to $2,000 used. Budget $300 to $800 for branding, packaging, and a delivery vehicle setup for local drop-offs. Wholesale flower accounts often require a minimum order, so budget for slightly larger initial purchases. A simple booking and invoicing system costs $20 to $50 a month and helps manage event deposits. Set aside a buffer for flower waste, since perishable stock does not always sell before it wilts. Delivery supplies like water tubes, floral sleeves, and insulated boxes add a small but steady monthly cost. A simple point-of-sale system for market days helps track which arrangements sell fastest at each event. Many bouquet makers also budget for basic photography props and a backdrop. Strong product photos drive most online orders on Etsy and Instagram. A small ribbon and card supply for personalized notes adds a nice touch for gift orders. Some makers also budget for seasonal decor, like dried flowers or greenery, to extend their offerings beyond fresh stems.

## Writing a Business Plan

Define your target customer, whether that is everyday gift buyers, brides, or corporate event planners. Plan your sourcing relationships with wholesalers or local flower farms for consistent, fresh supply. Estimate weekly order volume needed to cover flower costs and a fair profit. Set a launch goal for your first season, accounting for flower seasonality.

## Registering Your Business and Getting an EIN

Many bouquet makers start as a sole proprietorship. An LLC is worth it once you take wedding deposits or large orders. Register your business name with your state and apply for a free EIN through the IRS. This separates personal finances from business liability, which matters for event contracts. Open a business bank account before accepting deposits for weddings or events.

## Funding Your Launch

Most bouquet businesses start with personal savings given the relatively low equipment needs. Presales or deposits for wedding bookings can help fund inventory for larger events. A small business credit card can bridge flower purchases before payment arrives. Avoid large loans until recurring order volume is established.

## Pricing Your Bouquets

Calculate flower cost, container cost, and your time, then apply a markup that covers waste from unsold stems. Wedding and event work should be priced separately with a deposit and final balance structure. Compare your pricing to local florists, since handmade bouquets can often command a premium for personalization. Adjust pricing seasonally, since flower costs spike around holidays like Valentine's Day and Mother's Day.

## Where to Find Your First Customers

Instagram and Pinterest are essential for bouquet businesses since the product is entirely visual. Etsy works well for gift bouquets and dried arrangements shipped nationally. Local wedding fairs and vendor directories connect you with brides planning events months in advance. Partnering with local event planners and photographers generates steady referral business. Offering a small discount for repeat subscription customers can build predictable recurring revenue. Attending local bridal shows, even as a small vendor, can generate several bookings from a single event.

## Growing and Hiring Help

Once wedding season books up months in advance, bring on a part-time assistant for prep and delivery. Consider adding a subscription bouquet service for recurring weekly or monthly revenue. Wholesale relationships with local boutiques or cafes can expand your everyday bouquet sales. Track which arrangement styles and price points sell fastest to refine your catalog. Standardizing your most popular arrangements makes it easier to train an assistant without sacrificing quality. Revisit your supplier relationships each season, since flower availability and pricing shift constantly.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Ordering too much perishable inventory before demand is proven is a common early mistake that eats directly into profit. Underpricing wedding work is another frequent trap, since brides often expect custom quotes that account for real labor time. Skipping a written contract for event bookings can lead to disputes over deposits or last-minute changes. Relying on a single flower wholesaler without a backup plan can leave you without stock during a busy week.

A handmade bouquet business rewards consistency in quality and sourcing more than any single big event.
