Pressure washing is one of the lowest-cost service businesses to launch, often startable for a few thousand dollars in equipment. Demand is steady because homeowners and property managers need driveways, siding, roofs, and fleets cleaned on a recurring basis. Many operators report reaching profitability within their first year, since overhead stays low and jobs are priced per project.

## Validating Demand in Your Market

Look at your local market before buying equipment. Check how many pressure washing companies already serve your zip code and what services they emphasize. Search Google and Facebook groups for complaints about slow response times or inconsistent quality. A gap in reliability is often easier to exploit than a gap in price.

Talk to a few property managers about how often they currently get exteriors cleaned. Consider seasonality in your region as well, since demand for driveway and siding cleaning often spikes in spring and fall. Some operators shift toward holiday light installation or snow-related services in winter to keep revenue steady. Mapping out these seasonal swings early helps you plan staffing and cash flow instead of reacting to a slow month.

## Equipment You Actually Need to Start

A basic setup with a gas pressure washer, surface cleaner, hoses, and safety gear can run $1,500 to $4,000. A truck-mounted hot water system for commercial contracts costs significantly more, often $10,000 or above. Many new owners start with a trailer-mounted cold water unit and upgrade once they land recurring commercial accounts.

Hot water systems clean grease and oil stains far better, which matters for restaurant patios and commercial kitchens. Cold water works fine for most residential driveways and siding jobs. Budget separately for insurance, fuel, and chemicals each month. These recurring costs add up quickly once you are running several jobs a week.

## Registering the Business and Getting an EIN

Most pressure washing operators start as an LLC to separate personal assets from business liability. This work involves ladders, chemicals, and client property. Register your LLC with your state and apply for a free EIN through the IRS website. An EIN lets you open a business bank account and hire help later without using your personal Social Security number. Keeping business and personal finances separate from day one also makes tax season far less stressful.

## Licensing, Permits, and Insurance

Pressure washing itself is rarely licensed at the state level, but many cities require a general business license. General liability insurance is essential, since you are working near windows, cars, and landscaping that can be damaged. If you plan to use surface cleaners that discharge wastewater into storm drains, check your local environmental rules. Some municipalities require wastewater reclamation for commercial jobs.

Workers' compensation coverage becomes necessary once you hire your first employee in most states. If you plan to work on roofs or two-story homes, review your insurance policy for height restrictions. Do this before booking that type of job. Some commercial property managers will not sign a contract until they see a certificate of insurance. It must name them as additionally insured.

## Typical Startup Costs and Funding

Beyond your core equipment, plan for a reliable truck or trailer, business insurance, and a website or booking system. Total startup costs for a solo operator often land between $4,000 and $12,000. The exact figure depends on whether you buy new or used equipment. Because startup costs are relatively low compared to other trades, many owners self-fund with savings or a small personal loan.

Equipment financing through the manufacturer or a local bank is common for hot water units. These systems cost significantly more upfront. A business line of credit can smooth out cash flow between invoicing commercial clients and covering fuel costs. Avoid high-interest merchant cash advances if you can help it, since they can erode thin early margins quickly.

## Pricing Your Services

Residential jobs are typically priced per square foot or as a flat rate for driveways, siding, and decks. Commercial contracts often move to a monthly or quarterly recurring schedule with negotiated rates. Research three to five local competitors and price near the middle initially. Raise your rates once your calendar fills and referrals start coming in.

Bundling services, such as driveway plus siding plus gutter cleaning, increases your average ticket size per visit. Offering a modest discount for recurring seasonal contracts encourages repeat bookings. Customers book you again automatically instead of shopping around.

## Finding Your First Customers

Door hangers in target neighborhoods still work well for driveway and siding jobs. Before-and-after photos posted to a local Facebook group or Nextdoor generate strong word of mouth. Cold outreach to property management companies and HOAs can produce larger recurring contracts. Google Business Profile listings with reviews matter heavily for local search visibility.

Offering a small discount for the first three neighbors on a street who book together can generate quick momentum. Local trade shows for property managers and real estate agents are another underused channel for landing recurring commercial work. A simple referral bonus for existing customers who send you a new client often pays for itself many times over.

## Common Mistakes New Operators Make

Underpricing jobs to win early customers is the most common mistake. It is hard to raise rates on existing clients later. Skipping insurance to save money is another frequent error that can end a business after a single damage claim. Many new owners also neglect scheduling software, leading to double bookings and missed appointments during busy seasons.

## Hiring and Scaling Your Crew

Once you are consistently booked out two or more weeks, it is time to add a second crew. Hire helpers who can be trained quickly on surface cleaning and soft washing techniques. Standardize your quoting process so new hires price jobs consistently across every crew.

Investing in scheduling software early makes it much easier to coordinate multiple crews across different job sites. Track job costing closely as you scale. Fuel, chemicals, and labor eat into margins faster than most new owners expect. A second truck-mounted rig is usually the next major purchase once one crew alone cannot keep up with demand. Consider adding adjacent services like gutter cleaning or window washing to increase revenue per stop.
