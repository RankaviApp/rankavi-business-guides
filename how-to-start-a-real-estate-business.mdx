Real estate remains one of the largest asset classes in the economy. Residential transactions alone generate tens of billions of dollars in commission income each year in the U.S. Real estate has a clear, state-mandated path to entry through licensing, which creates both a barrier and a credibility signal. Agents who treat it as a real business, not a side hustle, tend to outlast market cycles.

## Getting Licensed

Every state requires a real estate license, earned through pre-licensing coursework, a state exam, and sponsorship by a licensed broker. Course hours range from about 40 to over 180 depending on the state. Agents must complete continuing education, usually every one to two years, to renew. Many states also require a post-licensing course in your first year. A broker's license needs additional experience hours and a separate exam.

## Choosing Your Niche

Decide whether you want residential, commercial, investment properties, or eventually your own brokerage. Study your local market's inventory levels, average days on market, and commission norms. Talk to working agents about realistic first-year income. Most new agents earn little in their first six to twelve months. Narrowing to a specific neighborhood or property type speeds up how fast you build a reputation.

## Writing a Business Plan

Treat yourself as a business from day one, even while working under a broker. Define your target niche, such as first-time buyers or relocation clients. Set a lead generation plan with specific weekly activities. Budget for the gap between licensing and your first closed commission, which can run several months.

## Registering the Business and Getting an EIN

Once licensed, most agents operate as independent contractors under a brokerage. Many form an LLC or S-corp for tax purposes once income grows, since commissions are 1099 income. Get an EIN from the IRS if you form an entity or plan to hire a team. Choosing the right brokerage matters, since commission splits and brand recognition vary enormously between firms.

## MLS Access and Association Dues

MLS membership and REALTOR association dues are ongoing costs tied to practicing legally in most markets. MLS access typically runs $20 to $100 or more a month depending on your region. Association dues often add $300 to $600 a year. Errors and omissions insurance, sometimes partly covered by your brokerage, generally costs $300 to $1,000 annually.

## Typical Startup Costs

Getting licensed typically costs $500 to $1,500 for coursework, exams, and background checks. Ongoing costs include MLS access, association dues, a website, and a CRM tool. Marketing materials and professional photography for listings add expense. Many new agents budget $3,000 to $10,000 for their first year before commission income steadies out.

## Funding Your First Year

Most new agents self-fund their first year from savings, since commission income is irregular. Some brokerages offer training stipends or reduced desk fees in exchange for a higher commission split. A part-time job or a spouse's income during ramp-up is common and worth planning for honestly.

## Understanding How Commissions Work

Your income comes from commission splits, not hourly pricing, typically a percentage of the sale price. Commission structures shifted after 2024 industry settlements, so buyer agreements with disclosed compensation are now standard. Your split with your brokerage might range from 50/50 for new agents to 90/10 for established producers.

## Finding Your First Clients

Your sphere of influence, meaning friends, family, and past coworkers, generates most of a new agent's first deals. Open houses and local Facebook or Nextdoor groups build visibility in your neighborhood. Online lead services can help too, though conversion rates trail personal referrals. Consistent follow-up over months, not days, actually converts most real estate leads.

## Building Your Online Presence

A professional headshot, a clean personal website, and an active social media presence are table stakes for new agents today. Video content showing local neighborhoods and market updates performs particularly well and builds familiarity before a prospect ever calls. Google Business Profile matters even for agents under a brokerage, since local searches for "realtor near me" happen constantly. Consistent, authentic content beats occasional polished posts, so pick a cadence you can actually sustain long term.

## Understanding Local Market Dynamics

Every real estate market moves on its own local rhythm. National trend headlines rarely tell you what you actually need to know. Track your specific zip codes closely, watching inventory levels, price-per-square-foot trends, and days on market. Attend local planning meetings or follow zoning changes, since new development affects both pricing and your talking points with clients. Genuine local expertise, not just a general license, separates agents who close consistently. The rest tend to just wait for luck. Clients notice quickly when an agent can speak specifically about a neighborhood's schools, traffic patterns, and upcoming projects.

## Working With a Mentor or Team Lead

Many top-producing agents credit an early mentor with cutting years off their learning curve. Some brokerages formally pair new agents with an experienced producer for a percentage of the first few deals. Even an informal arrangement, shadowing showings and negotiations, teaches lessons no course can replicate. Ask direct questions about handling difficult negotiations, pricing conversations, and managing client expectations under stress. The right mentor relationship pays for itself many times over in avoided early mistakes and faster deal flow.

## Common Mistakes New Agents Make

Many new agents underestimate how long the first sale actually takes and run out of savings too early. Skipping a genuine business plan in favor of just waiting for referrals leaves too much to chance. Spending heavily on paid leads before mastering your sphere of influence often wastes a limited early marketing budget. Failing to track follow-up consistently is one of the most common, and most fixable, reasons deals slip away.

## Hiring and Scaling When You're Ready

As your pipeline grows, a transaction coordinator handling paperwork frees you up for client-facing work. Successful agents eventually build a small team of buyer's agents under their brand. Scaling in real estate is largely about systematizing lead generation and follow-up.
