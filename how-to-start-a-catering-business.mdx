Event and catered food spending has stayed resilient. Weddings, corporate events, and private parties keep relying on outside caterers rather than in-house cooking. Catering rewards culinary skill with genuinely strong margins on volume events, unlike a restaurant carrying constant overhead. It's also one of the more regulation-heavy food businesses to start, so getting permits right early matters.

## Licensing and Permits Catering Requires

Catering is one of the most heavily regulated niches in this guide. You'll need a food service license from your local health department, which typically requires a kitchen inspection. Most states require a licensed commercial kitchen, not a home kitchen. Some allow limited home-based catering under cottage food laws. At least one staff member needs a food handler's or ServSafe certification.

## Building Vendor and Rental Relationships

Reliable relationships with rental companies for linens, tables, and dishware make scaling to larger events far easier. Negotiate standing rates with a produce supplier or wholesale food distributor once you're ordering consistently. A backup vendor for key ingredients protects you from last-minute shortages before a major event. Build a small network of reliable part-time staff you can call on short notice. It matters just as much as your food supply chain. Treat these relationships as seriously as your client relationships, since a vendor failure on event day becomes your problem instantly.

## Choosing Your Event Focus

Study local catering competitors and note gaps, whether a specific cuisine, dietary specialty, or event size they're not serving well. Talk to event planners and past clients about what makes them choose one caterer over another. Decide whether you'll focus on weddings, corporate lunches, or both, since staffing and menus differ significantly.

## Writing a Business Plan

Define your menu style, target event size, and service model, whether drop-off or full-service with staff. Target a food cost percentage of 28 to 35 percent of your menu price. Build staffing costs into every quote too. Plan how many events per month you need to hit your revenue goal.

## Registering the Business and Getting an EIN

Form an LLC given the liability exposure in food service, from foodborne illness claims to event accidents. Register with your state and apply for a free EIN from the IRS. You'll need it for a business bank account and vendor accounts. Venues and corporate clients often require your business entity name before booking you.

## Insurance Requirements

General liability insurance is essential, and many venues require a minimum coverage amount naming you as additional insured. A separate liquor liability policy and state alcohol permit are required if you serve alcohol. Equipment insurance is worth carrying once you own chafing dishes and transport vehicles worth protecting.

## Typical Startup Costs

Catering startup costs vary widely, typically $10,000 to $50,000. Renting a licensed commercial kitchen by the hour, often $15 to $40, avoids a major buildout expense. Chafing dishes, serving equipment, and transport containers run $2,000 to $8,000. A catering van adds $5,000 to $25,000 if you don't already have one.

## Funding Options

Many caterers test their menu through farmers markets or pop-up events before committing to full buildout costs. An SBA microloan can cover commercial kitchen rental and initial equipment for full operations. Some caterers partner with an existing restaurant to use their kitchen during off-hours, cutting startup capital significantly.

## Pricing Your Catering Services

Catering is typically priced per person. Drop-off catering runs $15 to $35 a head, while full-service plated dinners run $75 or more. Calculate food cost, labor, rentals, and transportation before setting a per-person price. Minimum event pricing protects you from bookings too small for the setup effort involved.

## Finding Your First Customers

Wedding and event planners are one of the strongest referral sources, since they book repeatedly and refer trusted vendors. Getting on a venue's preferred vendor list generates consistent inbound leads. Instagram and TikTok showcasing plated dishes drive strong organic interest for catering specifically. Tastings for corporate event planners convert larger recurring accounts.

## Menu Development and Tastings

Your menu is your primary sales tool, so treat tastings as seriously as the events themselves. Offer a focused set of proven dishes rather than an overwhelming, all-things-to-everyone menu. Seasonal ingredients keep costs lower and menus feeling fresh throughout the year. For corporate tastings, present two or three tiered package options. That lets the client choose within a budget range instead of starting from scratch. Photograph every tasting and event plate professionally, since strong food photography is what actually gets a caterer booked.

## Managing Staffing for Event Day

Event-day staffing is where many new caterers underestimate their true labor needs. A general rule is one server per ten to fifteen guests for plated service, and fewer for casual buffet-style events. Build a reliable roster of part-time servers and bartenders you can call on short notice for larger bookings. Brief your staff thoroughly before every event, covering the menu, timeline, and any allergy or dietary restrictions. Consistent staff briefings prevent the kind of on-site confusion that damages a caterer's reputation faster than almost anything else.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Underpricing per-person rates to win a wedding booking often means losing money once real labor and rentals are counted. Skipping a detailed contract on headcount changes and cancellation policy invites disputes close to event day. New caterers sometimes overbuy perishable ingredients for a first large event, cutting deeply into margin if guest counts shift. Neglecting food safety documentation can also jeopardize your health permit during a routine inspection.

## Handling Contracts and Deposits

A signed contract with a clear deposit structure protects you from last-minute cancellations that leave you holding purchased ingredients. Most caterers require a non-refundable deposit, often 25 to 50 percent, to hold an event date. Spell out final headcount deadlines, typically one to two weeks before the event, since kitchens need lead time to prep. Include a clause for weather contingencies on outdoor events, since rain plans affect both your setup and your staffing. A clear, professional contract also signals to corporate clients that you run a serious, reliable operation.

## Hiring and Scaling When You're Ready

Once you're booking multiple events a month, bring on part-time event staff rather than cooking and serving solo. Cross-train a second cook so you're not the sole point of failure for every event. Many catering businesses scale into a dedicated commissary kitchen once volume justifies it.
